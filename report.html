  1. Stacked ensemble is the most accurate. It has the lowest cross-validation error (RMSLE 0.109), and the hold-out confirms it: a typical error of 6.9%, with 81% of houses priced within 10% of what they sold for.
  2. XGBoost is statistically level with it. On the hold-out the two differ by less than the resampling noise (XGBoost does better in 43% of 2,000 bootstrap resamples). It trains in 0.74 s instead of 26 s, about 35× faster, which makes it the practical choice.
  3. Plain linear regression comes within 0.7 points. At 7.7% it shows that most of the accuracy comes from the inputs: log floor areas, quality scores and neighborhood. The more complex models add the last stretch.
  4. Decision tree is the weakest trained model at 12.0%. A single tree predicts in steps, one price per leaf, so it cannot follow a smooth price-per-square-foot trend.
  5. The cheapest homes are the hardest to price. For houses under $124k the winner's error is 11.9%, against 5.7% across the other price bands, and every model shows the same pattern.

How the test was run

Data

The Ames Housing data set (Dean De Cock, 2011; OpenML id 43926): 2,930 residential sales in Ames, Iowa, from 2006 to 2010, with 80 descriptive columns. Following De Cock's own advice, the 5 houses above 4,000 sq ft of living area were removed, so the results apply to homes up to that size. Quality ratings were turned into ordered scores (Poor = 1 to Excellent = 5), and seven columns were added: total floor area, bathroom count, age at sale, years since remodel, porch area, quality × living area, and a flag for missing street frontage. Prices are not adjusted for inflation.

Protocol

An 80/20 split, stratified by price, gave 2,340 training and 585 hold-out houses. Each model was tuned by random search with 5-fold cross-validation on the training houses only (the number of settings tried is in the table below). The winner was chosen on cross-validation alone. Only then was each model scored on the hold-out, once. All models learn log(price), so the training objective treats a 10% miss the same at any price.

The models

  • Stacked ensemble. Feeds out-of-fold predictions from the tuned elastic net, random forest, XGBoost and neural network into a linear blender with non-negative weights.
  • XGBoost. Gradient-boosted shallow trees, each one correcting the errors of the ones before.
  • Neural network. A small multilayer perceptron (one or two hidden layers, at most 128 units).
  • Elastic net. Linear regression with L1 + L2 penalties that shrink and drop weak features.
  • Linear regression. Ordinary least squares on log price. No tuning, no regularisation.
  • Random forest. 300 deep trees on bootstrap samples, averaged.
  • Decision tree. One tree of yes/no splits. Easy to read, prone to memorising the data.
  • Median price. Predicts the median training price for every house. Anything useful has to beat it.

What the numbers mean

MAPE
Mean absolute percentage error: the typical miss as a share of the sale price.
Within ±10%
Share of houses whose prediction lands within 10% of the sale price.
RMSLE
Root mean squared error of log price. Roughly a percentage error that punishes big misses more. It was the selection metric, and the Kaggle house-price competition uses it too.
MAE
Mean absolute error in dollars: the average miss.
RMSE
Root mean squared error in dollars. Dominated by the largest misses, which are mostly on expensive houses.
R²
Share of the variation in sale prices the model explains. 1.0 is perfect; 0 is no better than the average price.
CV
Cross-validation: train on four fifths of the training houses, score on the last fifth, five times over.
95% interval
From 2,000 bootstrap resamples of the hold-out houses. The same resamples are used for every model, so gaps between models are compared fairly.

Limits

  • One town and five years of sales. Expect larger errors on other markets or recent prices.
  • Sale type and condition (for example foreclosures, or new homes sold before completion) are inputs. They are known at sale time, but not when valuing a house that is not on the market.
  • Cross-validation scores of tuned models are slightly optimistic, because the same folds chose the settings. The hold-out numbers do not have this bias.
  • Timings are fits without parallel jobs (n_jobs=1) on one arm64 machine with scikit-learn 1.9.1, XGBoost 3.4.1 and Python 3.13.15. They show relative cost, not what a tuned production setup would take.
